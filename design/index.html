Vernacular Planarism

Vernacular Planarism is a visual practice concerned with isolating what remains meaningful after memory has discarded the incidental.

Over a lifetime of wandering, studying, traveling, making, and observing, certain things remain: a weathered barn at dusk, a spindle on a wooden sewing table, a roadside sign, a heron landing unexpectedly close, the diagram of cow breeds in a 1970s homesteading magazine, tar and sand and the smell of an Atlantic boardwalk, a one-off walnut guitar in a cramped music store, a fragment of medieval ornament nearly lost inside a larger exhibition.

Most encounters disappear. A few survive. What survives is rarely the detail. It is the structure—the outline, the proportion, the relationship, the gesture, the thing that made the encounter memorable in the first place.

The work begins with those surviving forms.

Vernacular Planarism reduces its subjects to geometric planes, hard boundaries, simplified silhouettes, and deliberately limited visual information. This is not minimalism, nor an attempt to imitate a particular regional style or artist. It is an attempt to approximate the way remembered things actually persist: detail dissolves, distractions disappear, and essential relationships remain.

The subjects are drawn from ordinary cultural life—architecture, tools, animals, craft traditions, natural history, music, working objects, signs, structures, and small remnants of human effort. They are things encountered rather than things selected according to a single geographic or aesthetic program.

The resulting images exist somewhere between artifact, monument, recollection, and sign. They are not intended to document an object exactly. They are attempts to preserve the shape of the experience of encountering it.

Vernacular Planarism is ultimately an act of recognition: a search for the essential structures and signifiers that remain after memory has finished its work.

Provision1 Visual Systems

Selected works are also used as development and production test assets for Provision1's visual-processing systems.

The portfolio therefore serves two purposes: it presents the visual work itself and provides real-world material for testing image conditioning, color handling, vector integrity, substrate preparation, and screen-to-print workflows.

AI-generated works are identified where applicable. Selected projects include processing documentation produced by the Provision1 prepress engines.

The four-pane audit view provides a compact record of the transformation: source artwork, conditioned master, differential analysis, and production simulation.
